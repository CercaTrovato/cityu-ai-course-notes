Supervised learning
Supervised learning is a type of machine learning in which the model is trained on 
labeled
 data. Each training example consists of an input and a known correct output (the label). The algorithm learns the mapping from inputs to outputs so that it can predict the correct label for new, unseen data.

=== SLIDE 12 [ppt/slides/slide12.xml] ===
Supervised learning – Example
The problem
Finance teams receive thousands of invoices and credit-card transactions. Manually coding each item to the correct expense account (e.g., Travel, Office Supplies, Marketing, Utilities) is time-consuming and inconsistent.
How supervised learning helps
Historical invoices that accountants have already coded become the 
labeled
 training data.
Features used by the model may include vendor name, invoice description, amount, department, and date.
The model learns patterns such as “Uber or taxi receipts → Travel” or “Adobe subscription → Software / IT.”
New invoices are automatically suggested a code

=== SLIDE 16 [ppt/slides/slide16.xml] ===
Unsupervised learning – Example
The problem
Large organizations generate millions of journal entries. Manual review of every entry is impossible. Traditional rule-based filters (e.g., “amounts over $10,000”) miss sophisticated or smaller but unusual items.
How unsupervised learning helps
The model learns what “normal” looks like across dimensions such as amount, account combinations, timing, user, and description.
It then flags entries that are statistical outliers or that form unusual clusters.
No prior 
labeling
 of fraudulent entries is required — the system discovers deviations on its own.

=== SLIDE 20 [ppt/slides/slide20.xml] ===
Semi-supervised learning – Example
The problem
A company may have thousands of contracts, but experts only have time to carefully review and label a few hundred. Fully manual classification is impractical; fully unsupervised methods may lack the precision needed for accounting judgments.
How semi-supervised learning helps
Experts label a representative sample of contracts (the 
labeled
 set).
The model uses these labels plus the remaining 
unlabeled
 contracts to learn better decision boundaries.
Features may include extracted clauses, parties, payment terms, duration, and keywords.
The resulting model can classify the remaining contracts with higher accuracy.

=== SLIDE 24 [ppt/slides/slide24.xml] ===
Reinforcement learning – Example
The problem
Traditional audit sampling often uses fixed sample sizes or simple random selection. In complex populations, this can be inefficient: too much effort may be spent on low-risk areas while higher-risk items are under-sampled.
How reinforcement learning concepts apply
The “agent” is the sampling strategy.
The “environment” is the population of transactions or accounts.
Actions include choosing which item to examine next.
Rewards can be defined in terms of error detection rate, coverage of high-risk items, or efficiency (errors found per hour of work).
Over successive audits or simulations, the strategy learns to allocate effort more effectively.
24

It is mathematically represented by the following equation: 
Y= 
a+b
*X + e
Where,
a = intercept,  b = slope,  e = error term

=== SLIDE 43 [ppt/slides/slide43.xml] ===
The evolution of AI in accounting
Manual Bookkeeping Era
: For decades, accounting relied on detail-oriented individuals to prepare financial statements manually.
Spreadsheet Introduction (1980s)
: Tools like VisiCalc, Lotus 1-2-3, and Microsoft Excel automated basic tasks like data entry and expense management, laying the groundwork for future advancements.
OCR Technology Adoption (1980s)
: Optical character recognition (OCR) facilitated data extraction from invoices, streamlining workflows.
Early AI Applications (1990s)
: Artificial neural networks supported tasks like fraud detection and financial forecasting, though their adoption was limited due to complexity.
AI Growth Phase (2000s)
: Improved machine learning algorithms enhanced forecasting, analysis, and risk assessment, leading to wider AI adoption in accounting.
Rise of NLP-Enhanced Tools (2010s and Beyond)
: Natural language processing allowed AI to interpret human language, generate reports, 
analyze
 real-time financial data, and support decision-making with predictive insights.

=== SLIDE 45 [ppt/slides/slide45.xml] ===
Disadvantages of AI in accounting
Learning
Transitioning to AI-powered software can unlock significant benefits, but it may require leaving behind familiar platforms and learning new ones, which can feel daunting.
Training
Investing time in training is essential, and costs can rise if you choose specialist training or are transitioning an entire practice.
Switching Costs
Adopting new technology often leads to an initial dip in productivity as users adjust to new processes. While the payback isn't immediate, it typically follows quickly.
Quality Control
While automation is powerful, relying too heavily on auto-suggestions can lead to errors. Users must focus on reviewing these suggestions. Fortunately, software tools often include features like find-and-recode to help correct mistakes.
