Course purpose

This course has two parts. In Classes 1–5, students use AI-assisted coding in R and Python to obtain
ﬁnancial data, construct predictors, study return predictability, compare linear and nonlinear models,
interpret model outputs, and turn forecasts into portfolio decisions. You will not be asked to memorize
code. You will be asked to deﬁne the problem, check when each variable became available, protect the
validation and test samples, and report weak results as weak.
Classes 6–12 study how FinTech businesses work: the technology they use, how they make money, the
risks they create, and how they are regulated. We cover blockchain, Bitcoin and Ethereum, decentralized
ﬁnance, tokenization, stablecoins and central bank digital currencies, payment systems, lending, robo-
advising, cybersecurity, RegTech, and selected AI applications for ﬁnancial and compliance text.
Classes 1–5 build on each other, so regular attendance is important.
Learning outcomes

The course aims to help students:

 1. use selected AI-assisted R and Python workﬂows to organize, visualize, and check the timing of
    ﬁnancial data;
 2. interpret and compare selected regression and machine-learning methods for return prediction and
    portfolio applications using out-of-sample evidence, including the limits of coeﬃcients, feature im-
    portance, and response proﬁles;
 3. explain core mechanisms and business models in selected FinTech areas and recognize major ﬁnancial,
    operational, cybersecurity, conduct, and regulatory risks; and
 4. communicate an evidence-based analysis of a FinTech company or ﬁnancial machine-learning appli-
    cation.

Coverage and depth depend on class progress. Students are assessed only on material taught in class or
released through Canvas.

FinTech company case project. Teams of up to three students submit a written case report (10%,
no more than ten pages) and a recorded video presentation (20%, no more than ten minutes) in Week 12.
Every member must deliver at least two minutes of substantive presentation. Submit one MP4 ﬁle no
larger than 80 MB. There is no in-person presentation. A separate brief gives the framework, evidence
standards, and rubric.
Machine-learning investment assignment. Teams of up to three submit one joint report, portfolio-
weight CSV, and reproducible code package. It is released after the Week 5 class and is due two weeks
later, in Week 7. The report cover lists all team members and CityU student numbers; one member is
the designated submitter. The assignment brief gives the required analysis, submission instructions, and
grading criteria.
Teaching-assistant tutorials. Two of those Sunday slots are run by a teaching assistant: 27 Septem-
ber 2026 on preparing the machine-learning assignment, and 29 November 2026 on preparing for the
examination. Both are recorded.
Participation. Everyone starts at ﬁve marks out of ten and the mark moves during the semester.

• Cold call. I call on students by name rather than wait for volunteers, so expect to be asked.
• A correct or well-reasoned answer earns one mark. Sound reasoning counts even when the
  ﬁnal answer is not the expected one.
• A wrong answer costs nothing. Being wrong, or passing, leaves the mark unchanged.
• Being absent when called costs one mark. Email me at least a day ahead if you cannot attend
  your registered class; otherwise the mark falls by one.

Questions, short exercises, and useful feedback to classmates count toward the same ten marks.

Final examination. The examination covers both course blocks and tests concepts, mechanisms, cal-
culations, and critical comparison. It is handwritten and held during the examination period. A mock
paper is released on Canvas in mid-November, and the second teaching-assistant tutorial works through
it. Permitted materials will be conﬁrmed on Canvas, subject to the ﬁnal University examination instruc-
tions.

Course practices and policies


• Evidence. Time-sensitive claims about markets, companies, and regulation must be supported by a
  dated and credible source.
• Reproducibility. Empirical work must preserve the data timing and transformation steps needed
  to reproduce the reported result.
• Generative AI. AI-assisted coding is used in Classes 1–5. AI may be used in coursework when
  permitted by the relevant instructions and disclosed. Students remain responsible for all submitted
  work. Generative-AI tools are not permitted in the ﬁnal examination.
• Academic integrity. Students must submit their own work, acknowledge others’ words, graphics,
  ideas, and data, and report ﬁndings honestly. Plagiarism, fabrication, undisclosed copying, and sub-
  mission of unveriﬁed generated material are serious oﬀences under the University’s Rules on Academic
  Honesty. All new students admitted in Semester A 2026/27 must complete the online tutorial and
  quiz and submit the declaration through the Academic Honesty website no later than 30 November
  2026.
• Announcements. Later Canvas or University announcements take precedence.
