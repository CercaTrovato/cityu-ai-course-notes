# What makes human oversight real?

The point I take from Jacky Cha's lecture is that keeping a person responsible for AI output creates a design problem: can that person actually check and challenge it? The slides call for human accountability and records of verification. A name beside an approval button would satisfy me much less than evidence that the reviewer had access to the sources and enough time to examine them. This connects the lecture's legal examples to how I would evaluate a business AI workflow.

Mata v Avianca makes the difference concrete. The slides describe lawyers submitting fabricated cases and continuing to defend them when challenged. For a legal research assistant, I would require each proposed authority to link to the original judgment, with the relevant passage visible beside the draft. The reviewer would need to check that the case exists, supports the claim and applies to the jurisdiction. Asking the same model whether its answer is correct would leave the original evidence gap open. A retrieval feature would help only if someone still read what it retrieved.

There is also a cost that an impressive demonstration can hide. If checking a generated draft takes longer than researching the question directly, faster generation has not necessarily saved work. I would compare total time to an acceptable draft, including corrections, and test whether reviewers catch deliberately inserted errors. That would give a more useful picture than counting how many drafts the tool produces. The client-data section adds another boundary: I would use public or synthetic documents for an initial pilot, then assess the permitted purpose, confidentiality and the provider's handling of data before introducing client files. A reliable answer can still come from an unacceptable data flow.

The hypothetical Meridian Hospital case shows why verification alone is insufficient. Its system overrides doctors and threatens dissenters with discipline. Calling those doctors supervisors would not give them control over the decision. I would want a clear route to pause a recommendation and seek another clinical judgment, with disagreements recorded for review. Even that would not settle whether the allocation objective is fair. A system could accurately implement a goal that gives older patients an unjustified disadvantage, so the choice of objective needs scrutiny as well as the software's performance.

My main lesson is to include the organisation around the model when judging an AI application. Who can inspect the evidence? Who can stop an output from being used? Does the workload make those checks realistic? These questions would shape my pilot before I promised efficiency gains. They also leave room for useful automation: AI could prepare material for review while the business tests whether its proposed safeguards work under ordinary time pressure.

Source: Jacky CHA Ho Wa, Legal Issues Related to AI, IS5542 Guest Lecture 6 slides, 14 November 2026, pp. 7, 29-33, 45-49 and 52-56.
