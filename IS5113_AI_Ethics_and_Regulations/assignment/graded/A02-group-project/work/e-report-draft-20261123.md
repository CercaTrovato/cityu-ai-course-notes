# DRAFT — Forecasting evidence and originality review in AI-assisted fashion

## Individual quality and shortlist diversity

A fashion brand should assess whether each AI-assisted concept meets its design brief and whether the shortlisted concepts explore meaningfully different possibilities. A shoe can satisfy its functional and aesthetic requirements while belonging to a shortlist that repeats the same silhouette. The ethical concern is whether designers retain room to consider and defend alternatives before a visually attractive output becomes the default choice.

Nike's account of A.I.R. provides a concrete starting point. The company reports that many early AI images interpreting Air shared a flowing visual style and describes designers selecting inspiration to develop distinct concepts (NIKE, Inc., 2024, Phase Two). This is a company account of concept development, rather than an independent assessment of originality in Nike's final footwear.

Independent studies help explain why separate assessments are useful. Wadinambiarachchi et al. (2024, §§3.1–3.2, 4.2) studied 60 participants sketching chatbot avatars in a 20-minute task. All conditions received the same example. Sketches produced with access to Midjourney V4 repeated more of the example's 14 tracked features on average than sketches produced without an additional inspiration tool. This measure of design fixation concerns reliance on a supplied example; it does not establish copying from Nike or a general loss of creativity in professional footwear design.

Doshi and Hauser (2024, arXiv v3, Figs. 2 and 5) examined a different outcome. In their experiment, 293 writers were assigned to write eight-sentence stories without AI ideas or with access to one or up to five GPT-4 ideas. Access improved some evaluations of individual stories, while stories were more similar to the average content of other stories in their own condition. These findings motivate checking individual quality and collective diversity separately. Their transfer to footwear remains a question for testing.

We propose that the design lead first review each concept against the brief, then compare the shortlist's silhouettes, construction and material choices. If alternatives differ mainly in colour, the lead should request a structurally different option or record why a common direction serves the brief. A shared style can be appropriate for a coherent collection; difference should not become a quota that displaces function or the intended identity. This review should make selection reasons visible and preserve designers' ability to challenge a default, rather than reward variation for its own sake.

## Source similarity and release decisions

Similarity to a reference requires a separate check from diversity within the shortlist. Carlini et al. (2023, §4.2, Fig. 3) extracted near-copies of training images from Stable Diffusion v1.4 through targeted generation and filtering. Their experiment establishes a reproduction risk in the tested setting, without estimating its frequency in ordinary fashion-design use or determining legal infringement.

When a proposed design closely resembles an identifiable source, we propose that the release lead hold the affected concept, preserve the reference and generation records, and refer the concern for source and permission review. The decision record should state the evidence, unresolved issues and reason for approval, revision or rejection. Clearance on similarity should not automatically clear cultural-use or production-impact concerns, which require their own reviews. Designers and source creators should have a route to raise a concern and receive a recorded response.

## Forecast review before production

Forecasting requires a different evidence boundary. GeoStyle estimates clothing attributes from Instagram and Flickr images grouped by city, then models how their prevalence changes over time (Mall et al., 2019, §3.1 and §4.1, Table 2). The observed photo sample is different from population-wide clothing preferences or demand for a particular product. A forecast could fit the available images while overlooking places or communities rarely photographed. Using that forecast to allocate stock could carry the coverage gap into which customers are served.

Nike's 2019 Celect announcement describes local demand prediction for inventory planning (NIKE, Inc., 2019). It does not establish that Nike uses GeoStyle, or that Celect supplies A.I.R. The reviewed sources support separate claims about demand sensing, image-based trend research and concept design.

We propose that a merchandise manager approve a production order only after the forecast owner identifies the target market, forecasted quantity, time horizon and available reference outcomes. Checks should cover data coverage, privacy, uncertainty and errors against the intended target where reliable data and enough observations exist. Demographic comparisons should use data that are lawfully obtained and sufficiently reliable; missing labels should be recorded rather than inferred from appearance. If the evidence cannot support the proposed use, the manager should limit the order, seek additional evidence or hold the decision, recording the reason.

These reviews impose time and staffing costs. A limited pilot could compare briefs reviewed with and without the proposed design procedure, recording individual brief fit, shortlist variation, review time and reasons for overrides. Forecast review could track errors and subsequent inventory outcomes using a consistent target and period. These are proposed evaluation steps; reductions in waste or improvements in Nike's originality have not been measured here.
